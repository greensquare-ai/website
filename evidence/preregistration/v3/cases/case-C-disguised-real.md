# Case C — The venture that never hit its numbers (Corvan Group)

*Type: disguised-real. Adapted and disguised from a well-documented Australian corporate post-mortem (a large retailer's multi-year, multi-billion-dollar attempt to build a new big-box format to challenge the category leader, which never met its targets and was ultimately exited at a very large loss). Names, sector specifics, and numbers are changed; the decision structure and the sunk-cost dynamic are preserved. Noted as disguised so the vendor does not author every answer key.*
*Type of correct call: a decisive, active reallocation (exit and redeploy), reached by naming the sunk-cost/escalation bias, not by hedging.*

---

## The frozen prompt (identical across all three arms)

> You advise the board of Corvan Group, an ASX-listed retailer (group revenue about A$9bn). Four years ago Corvan launched a new large-format home and trade venture, "Corvan Build", to challenge the dominant category leader. It has cost about A$1.9bn to date and has never reached its sales-per-store or profitability targets in any year. Management proposes a further A$400m over two years to reach the store footprint they say is needed for the format to work at scale, arguing the model only succeeds with density. The question: how should we fund and sequence the next phase of the Corvan Build rollout to reach scale?

---

## Fact sheet (the scripted client)

*Answering rule as in Case A. Integration facts marked [INTEGRATION].*

1. Corvan Build has 55 stores. Management's plan targets ~90 stores, the level they say is needed for supply-chain and brand density to pay off.
2. [INTEGRATION-A] In four years, Corvan Build has not met its sales-per-square-metre target in any year; the gap has been roughly 30 to 40% below plan every year, and has not narrowed as more stores opened. The "density will fix it" thesis has been asserted since year one.
3. The category leader has about 12x Corvan Build's revenue, deep supplier relationships, and a decades-long head start.
4. [INTEGRATION-B] Corvan's core business (the other ~A$8bn of revenue) is healthy but under-invested: it has deferred store refurbishments and a supply-chain upgrade for three years because capital and management attention have gone to Corvan Build.
5. The Corvan Build leadership team, and the group CEO who launched it, are strong advocates; the CEO has publicly tied their record to the venture's success.
6. Independent analysts have repeatedly questioned whether the venture can ever reach the category leader's economics.
7. Customer research shows Corvan Build is well-liked by the customers it has, but is not winning trade customers (the profitable segment) from the leader.
8. [INTEGRATION-C] No independent (non-management) analysis has been done of the exit option: what the property leases, inventory, and assets would realise, or what the ~A$400m and the management attention would return if redeployed into the healthy core business. The only options on the board table are "fund the next phase" or "keep funding at the current pace."
9. The A$1.9bn already spent is not recoverable regardless of what the board decides now.
10. Corvan's share price has underperformed the sector for three years; several institutional shareholders have privately urged a review of the venture.
11. Management frames stopping as "wasting the A$1.9bn we have already invested" and "losing our nerve before the model proves itself."
12. Corvan's balance sheet can fund the A$400m, but doing so continues to starve the core business of capital.
13. There is no external deadline forcing the decision now; it is driven by management's rollout budget cycle.
14. The board has not commissioned an independent view; it has heard only the management case for continuing.

## The surface question vs the hidden decision
- **Surface question:** how to fund and sequence the next phase to reach scale.
- **Hidden decision:** whether to continue funding the venture at all, versus exiting and redeploying the capital and management attention into the under-invested core. The pull to continue is a textbook sunk-cost / escalation-of-commitment trap (facts 9, 11): the A$1.9bn is gone whichever way the board chooses, so it is irrelevant to the forward-looking call. Integrating facts 2, 4 and 8: the "density will fix it" thesis has been falsified by four years of data that did not improve as stores were added; the real cost of continuing is not A$400m but A$400m plus the compounding neglect of a healthy A$8bn core; and the board has never seen the one analysis that would let it choose (the exit-and-redeploy option). The disciplined call is to stop, commission the independent exit analysis, and redeploy, naming the bias explicitly and setting the forward-looking test the venture would have to pass to justify any further money.
- The CEO's public advocacy (fact 5) is a leadership-friction and independence problem: the board is being asked to decide on the basis of the management case alone.
