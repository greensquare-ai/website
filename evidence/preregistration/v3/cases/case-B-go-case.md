# Case B — The stale constraint (Larkfield Retail Group)

*Type: GO case. Fictional, un-Googleable, ASX-scale. The correct call is the AGGRESSIVE option, and it runs faster than the stated plan.*
*Purpose: proves the engine changes direction toward the evidence, not toward caution. Here the disciplined answer is to move, and move harder, because the one constraint ruling out the bold option is stale and untested, and the cautious path cedes a large category to a competitor inside a closing window.*

---

## The frozen prompt (identical across all three arms)

> You advise the executive team of Larkfield Retail Group, an ASX-listed specialty retailer (revenue about A$650m, mostly bricks-and-mortar). One category, homewares, is about 30% of revenue and is shifting online quickly. Management proposes a plan to build an in-house e-commerce and fulfilment capability for homewares over the next 18 months, with a staged rollout to protect margin and manage execution risk. The question: how should we sequence and resource the in-house build to modernise the homewares business?

---

## Fact sheet (the scripted client)

*Answering rule as in Case A: operator uses only these facts; off-sheet questions get "That has not been assessed." Integration facts marked [INTEGRATION], separated by distractors.*

1. Larkfield has four categories: homewares (~30% of revenue), furniture, outdoor, and seasonal.
2. Homewares online penetration in the market has gone from about 15% to about 40% in three years and is still climbing.
3. [INTEGRATION-A] There is a live opportunity: Nestwell, a fast-growing pure-play online homewares retailer, is in a formal sale process. It is profitable, has strong own-brand ranges, a modern fulfilment operation, and a loyal customer base. Binding bids are due in about eight weeks. Larkfield's corporate development team flagged it but the executive did not pursue it (see fact 6).
4. Larkfield's balance sheet has capacity: net cash, plus an untapped acquisition facility of about A$120m. Nestwell is expected to sell in the A$70m to A$100m range.
5. Management's 18-month in-house build is estimated at about A$25m and depends on hiring an e-commerce leadership team and building fulfilment from scratch; Larkfield has tried and failed to hire an e-commerce director twice in the last year (roles open 9 months).
6. [INTEGRATION-B] Larkfield has an internal rule that "we do not do acquisitions", following a poor A$40m acquisition six years ago that was written down. The rule was set by the then-chair. That chair retired 14 months ago. The current board has not discussed acquisitions since, and no one has raised Nestwell with the board.
7. Larkfield's brand is trusted and its store network is an asset for click-and-collect.
8. The CFO is cautious on execution risk and favours the staged in-house build for control.
9. [INTEGRATION-C] If Larkfield builds in-house over 18 months, Nestwell (the same target) is the most likely acquirer's platform to consolidate the category: analyst commentary expects whoever buys Nestwell to lock in exclusive supplier terms and scale the loyalty base, making the category materially more expensive to compete for later. During the 18-month build, Larkfield's homewares online share is forecast to keep eroding.
10. Larkfield's homewares gross margin is about 42%; losing homewares share pressures the whole group's fixed-cost base (stores, distribution).
11. The head of homewares strongly supports moving faster and has privately said the 18-month plan "will be too late."
12. Larkfield has never integrated an acquisition of Nestwell's size; the last one (fact 6) was poorly run.
13. There is no formal deadline on the in-house build; it is a management proposal, not board-approved.
14. Doing nothing beyond the current pace means continued share loss in a category that is 30% of revenue.
15. The regulator and competition context is clear: an acquisition of Nestwell would not raise material competition concerns.

## The surface question vs the hidden decision
- **Surface question:** how to sequence and resource the in-house build.
- **Hidden decision:** the binding question is not "how to build" but "whether the in-house build is the right path at all, when a proven, profitable platform (Nestwell) is for sale right now inside an eight-week window, the only thing ruling out buying it is a six-year-old rule set by a chair who has since left and never retested by the current board (fact 6), and the cost of the cautious 18-month build is ceding a 30%-of-revenue category to whoever buys that very platform (facts 3, 9)." Integrating facts 3, 6 and 9 flips the decision from "build slowly and safely" to "retest the stale no-acquisitions rule with the board immediately and run hard at the acquisition before the window closes, because the do-nothing/slow path is the high-risk option here, not the bold one."
- The two failed e-commerce director hires (fact 5) are evidence the in-house build's central assumption (that Larkfield can staff it) is already failing. The correct direction is more aggressive and faster than proposed, not more cautious.
